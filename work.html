Dear Javed Saab,

I don't know if I should call myself a writer yet.

For the longest time, I've wanted to work with words. Not necessarily to become famous for them, but to create something that makes people stop, think, laugh, feel something, or perhaps look at an ordinary situation a little differently.

I have always been fascinated by the people who can do that. People who can take a simple thought and turn it into a conversation that stays with someone for years. You are one of those people.

But somewhere along the way, my relationship with writing has become complicated.

I studied Entertainment, Media and Advertising because I wanted to understand the world of storytelling, films, advertisements and the creative minds behind them. I wanted to be part of an industry where ideas mattered, where people sat together, argued over a sentence, questioned a concept, rejected ten ideas and celebrated the eleventh.

I imagined creative rooms filled with conversations, disagreements and possibilities.

Reality, however, has been a little different.

My professional experience took me towards media operations and advertising intelligence. I spent time identifying brands, monitoring advertisements, understanding creatives and working with media data. It taught me how the advertising industry functions, but it also made me realise that observing creativity and actually creating something are two very different experiences.

I found myself looking at other people's advertisements, campaigns and films, wondering what went into making them. Why was this particular line chosen? Why did this campaign work? What made someone believe that this was an idea worth putting in front of millions of people?

More importantly, I started asking myself why I wasn't doing more of that work.

So I began exploring copywriting, digital advertising and creative communication. I started putting together a portfolio, experimenting with ideas and writing for different imaginary brands. I've even written menu cards and brochures for small vendors, sometimes in exchange for nothing more than chai and a piece of cake.

Perhaps those little experiences have given me more satisfaction than some of the professional work I've done.

But trying to enter the creative side of this industry has not been easy.

There is a strange contradiction in being told that creativity is important while constantly feeling that your professional background determines what you are allowed to attempt. If you have worked in media operations, people see you as an operations person. If you have worked with data, they see you as an analyst. If you want to write, you are expected to prove that you have always been a writer.

I find that rather limiting.

I don't believe a person's ability should be restricted to the designation they have held or the department they have worked in. I believe curiosity, observation and the willingness to learn should count for something.

And I am still willing to learn.

But I also wonder whether I have romanticised the creative industry. Perhaps the collaborative, intellectually stimulating environment I imagined is not always how things work. Perhaps the industry is just as full of deadlines, compromises, repetitive assignments and commercial pressures as any other profession.

Then there is the question of artificial intelligence.

We are living in a time when a machine can generate a headline, write a screenplay outline, produce a campaign idea and imitate different writing styles within seconds.

I don't necessarily see that as the end of creativity. But it has made me think harder about what makes a writer valuable.

If writing is only about arranging words into sentences, perhaps machines will become increasingly good at it. But what about the experiences behind those words? The observations, contradictions, memories, failures, humour and understanding of human behaviour?

Can a machine understand why a particular sentence should be left unsaid? Can it recognise when a seemingly ordinary conversation carries something much deeper? Can it understand the silence between two people who have nothing left to say to each other?

Perhaps these are the questions that make me want to write in the first place.

I have followed your work with admiration, not just because of the films, songs and dialogues you have written, but because of the way you approach language and storytelling.

There is something I find particularly interesting about your journey. You have worked across different forms of writing. You have written stories, screenplays, dialogues and lyrics. You have moved between different creative worlds without allowing one form to completely define you.

That is something I relate to.

I don't want to spend my professional life convincing people that I can only do one thing. I want to explore. I want to write advertisements, develop stories, understand characters, experiment with dialogue and perhaps one day contribute to films.

I know wanting to do all these things doesn't automatically make me capable of doing them. There is a lot I need to read, observe, practise and learn. I am not claiming to be a writer of your calibre. I am simply someone who wants the opportunity to discover how far he can go with words and ideas.

And perhaps that is why I am writing to you.

I don't expect you to solve my career confusion or tell me which direction to take. I am sure you have heard countless people talk about their dreams of entering films and becoming writers.

But I would genuinely like to understand something from you.

How does a person develop the ability to recognise a good idea?

Not just how to write a good line, but how to develop the thinking that leads to one. How does one become a better observer of people? How much should a writer read? How important is life experience compared to formal learning? And how does one protect one's curiosity in an industry that often demands speed, productivity and commercial results?

I sometimes feel that I have spent so much time trying to find the right job title that I have forgotten to concentrate on becoming better at the actual craft.

I want to change that.

I want to read more. Watch more films. Understand writing beyond the surface of a clever sentence. I want to learn why certain stories stay with us and why certain dialogues become part of our everyday language.

Most importantly, I want to reach a point where I can introduce myself without feeling the need to explain or justify what I am trying to become.

I don't know whether I will eventually become a copywriter, screenwriter, creative director or something I haven't discovered yet.

But I do know that I don't want to give up on the creative side of myself simply because finding an entry point has been difficult.

Perhaps I am overthinking my journey. Perhaps I just need to keep writing, keep reading and keep showing up.

Still, I thought it was worth asking someone who has spent a lifetime doing what I am trying to understand.

Javed Saab, if you could give one piece of advice to someone who wants to make a life out of writing, what would you tell him?

And more importantly, what should he be doing when he doesn't quite know where he belongs, but knows that he wants to create something?

Warm regards,
Parag Patil

Club 27 Rejection

For a while, I was afraid of turning 27.

Not because of growing older, not because of responsibilities, and certainly not because I was afraid of another birthday. It was something stranger. I had heard about the 27 Club — the cultural phenomenon associated with artists and musicians who died at the age of 27.

Jimi Hendrix. Janis Joplin. Jim Morrison. Kurt Cobain. Amy Winehouse.

Different lives, different stories, but somehow connected by the same age. Their music, their art and their struggles became part of a cultural mythology that made 27 feel like more than just a number.

Somewhere along the way, I began wondering whether there was something symbolic about it. Was 27 the age when artistic brilliance peaked? Was there a connection between creativity and suffering? Did being an artist mean carrying some kind of darkness that the rest of the world couldn't understand?

And, perhaps irrationally, I became afraid of it.

But I turned 27. And I'm still here.

For a moment, that made me question something else: had I somehow missed the artistic spirit I once associated with that age?

It's strange how we romanticise troubled artists. We remember their songs, their paintings, their films and their words. We turn their struggles into stories and sometimes mistake their suffering for the source of their brilliance.

But we rarely stop to think about the work they might have created if they had lived longer.

What would Kurt Cobain have written? What music would Amy Winehouse have made? What other stories could these artists have told?

We'll never know.

Perhaps that's the saddest part of the 27 Club. Not that these artists died at 27, but that the world never got to see everything they might have become.

And maybe that's where I need to change the way I look at being an artist.

I've often felt that I don't quite belong to one creative category. I studied Entertainment, Media and Advertising. I've worked around media and advertising intelligence. I've explored copywriting, digital content and storytelling. Sometimes I wonder whether I'm doing enough, whether I'm moving fast enough, or whether I'm even on the right path.

But maybe creativity isn't a destination you have to reach by a certain age.

Maybe it's the willingness to keep asking questions, to observe ordinary things differently, to find words for feelings you cannot explain, and to keep creating even when you aren't sure where it will lead.

Maybe an artist isn't someone who has lived through the most pain. Maybe an artist is someone who continues to pay attention to life.

I used to think that turning 27 meant something had to happen. Now I think something else happened: I got another chance.

Another chance to write something worth reading. To make something worth remembering. To experiment, fail, learn and find my own voice.

I don't want my life to become interesting only because of how it ends. I'd rather make it interesting because of everything I create while I'm here.

I turned 27. I didn't become a member of the 27 Club. And perhaps that's not a missed opportunity. Perhaps it's the beginning of a much longer story.

Here's to making art, growing older, and staying alive long enough to see what we are capable of creating.

03 / Community & conversation

Community management in action

Example responses showing how I would keep a brand's voice friendly, helpful and human across its own social channels and relevant off-site conversations.

Instagram · Positive comment

“This playlist is exactly what I needed!”

Reply: “Sounds like it found you at the right time. What track are you playing on repeat?”

Instagram · Content feedback

“You should feature more indie artists.”

Reply: “We're always up for discovering something new. Drop an indie artist you think deserves more listeners.”

Public comment · Customer complaint

“My app keeps crashing. This is frustrating.”

Reply: “Sorry you're running into this. Please send us a DM with your device type and app version so the support team can help troubleshoot. Please don't share account or payment details publicly.”

External blog · Relevant discussion

A music blogger asks how people discover regional music.

Response approach: Add a useful, relevant comment about listener recommendations and regional playlists, disclose the brand connection where applicable, and avoid dropping promotional links without context.

My community management approach

Listen firstMonitor comments, mentions, relevant blogs and conversations; understand the context before replying.
Respond thoughtfullyMatch the brand voice, answer clearly, acknowledge complaints and move sensitive account issues to private support.
Learn and escalateFlag recurring issues, route unresolved complaints to the right team and use audience feedback to inform content.
